Throughout, rings have an identity $1$, subrings contain the same identity, and ring homomorphisms preserve it. Ideals are two-sided unless specified.

## Definitions

**Definition.** A *ring* is a set $R$ such that $(R,+)$ is an abelian group, multiplication is associative, and multiplication distributes over addition on both sides. A ring is *commutative* if multiplication commutes. A *division ring* is a nonzero ring in which every nonzero element is a unit; a commutative division ring is a *field*.

**Definition.** A *subring* $S\subseteq R$ contains $1_R$ and is closed under addition, additive inverses, and multiplication. A *unit* is $u\in R$ for which $uv=vu=1$ for some $v$; the units form the group $R^\times$.

**Definition.** The *characteristic* of $R$ is the least positive integer $n$ such that $n1_R=0$, or $0$ if none exists. Here $n1_R$ means a repeated sum, not ring multiplication by an element $n$.

## Polynomial rings

Let $R$ be commutative. The polynomial ring $R[x]$ consists of the formal sums

$$
f(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n,
$$

with the usual coefficientwise addition and convolution product. For $f\ne0$, $\deg f$ is its largest nonzero exponent; set $\deg0=-\infty$.

**Definition.** A *zero divisor* is a nonzero $a\in R$ for which $ab=0$ for some nonzero $b\in R$. An *integral domain* is a nonzero commutative ring with no zero divisors.

**Proposition.** $R[x]$ is an integral domain if and only if $R$ is an integral domain.

*Proof.* If $R$ is a domain and $f,g\ne0$, the leading coefficient of $fg$ is the product of two nonzero elements, so $fg\ne0$. Conversely, $R$ embeds as the constant polynomials, so zero divisors in $R$ remain zero divisors in $R[x]$. ◻

**Theorem.** **Division algorithm.** Let $f,g\in R[x]$, with $g\ne0$ and leading coefficient a unit. Then there are unique $q,r\in R[x]$ such that

$$
f=gq+r,\qquad r=0\quad\text{or}\quad\deg r<\deg g.
$$

In particular, this holds for every nonzero $g$ when $R$ is a field.

*Proof.* If $f=0$ or $\deg f<\deg g$, take $(q,r)=(0,f)$. Otherwise write $m=\deg f$, $n=\deg g$, $a=\operatorname{lc}(f)$, and $u=\operatorname{lc}(g)\in R^\times$, and set

$$
f_1=f-au^{-1}x^{m-n}g,
 \qquad \deg f_1<\deg f.
$$

Iteration gives $f=gq+r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<n$. If also $f=gq'+r'$, then

$$
g(q-q')=r'-r.
$$

If $q\ne q'$, then

$$
\deg(g(q-q'))=n+\deg(q-q')\ge n >\deg(r'-r),
$$

a contradiction. Hence $q=q'$ and $r=r'$. ◻

There is no ordering-free analogue for arbitrary multivariate division. Over a field, a chosen monomial order and an ordered list of divisors give the multivariate division algorithm; the remainder need not be unique unless additional hypotheses, such as a Gröbner basis, are imposed.

## Homomorphisms and ideals

**Definition.** A ring homomorphism $\phi:R\to S$ preserves $1$, addition, and multiplication. Its kernel is $\ker\phi=\\\{r:\phi\(r\)=0\\\}$. A bijective homomorphism is a ring isomorphism.

**Definition.** An *ideal* $I\trianglelefteq R$ is an additive subgroup such that $rI\subseteq I$ and $Ir\subseteq I$ for every $r\in R$. The ideal generated by $a_1,\dots,a_n$ is

$$
(a_1,\dots,a_n)=Ra_1R+\cdots+Ra_nR.
$$

For a commutative ring, $(a)=\\\{ra:r\in R\\\}$ is the *principal ideal* generated by $a$.

**Proposition.** Kernels of ring homomorphisms are ideals. Conversely, every ideal $I$ is the kernel of the quotient homomorphism $R\to R/I$.

*Proof.* Additivity makes a kernel an additive subgroup, and $\phi(ra)=\phi\(r\)\phi(a)=0=\phi(a)\phi\(r\)$ for $a$ in the kernel. For the quotient map, $r+I=I$ exactly when $r\in I$. ◻

**Proposition.** There is a unique ring homomorphism $\mathbb Z\to R$ sending $1$ to $1_R$. Its kernel is $n\mathbb Z$, where $n=\operatorname{char} R$.

*Proof.* The unique homomorphism is

$$
\eta_R:\mathbb Z\longrightarrow R,\qquad
m\longmapsto m1_R,\qquad
 \ker\eta_R=(\operatorname{char} R)\mathbb Z.
$$

 ◻

**Theorem.** **Universal property of a polynomial ring.** Let $R'$ be commutative, let $\phi:R\to R'$ be a homomorphism, and let $y\in R'$. There is a unique homomorphism $\Phi:R[x]\to R'$ extending $\phi$ and satisfying $\Phi(x)=y$, namely

$$
\Phi\left(\sum_i a_ix^i\right)=\sum_i\phi(a_i)y^i.
$$

Taking $y=x$ gives the coefficientwise extension $R[x]\to R'[x]$.

This substitution principle extends in the evident way to finitely many variables.

**Proposition.** A field has exactly the ideals $0$ and $F$. Consequently, every unital homomorphism from a field to a nonzero ring is injective.

*Proof.* If an ideal $I$ contains $a\ne0$, then $1=a^{-1}a\in I$, so $I=F$. The kernel of a unital map from $F$ is not all of $F$, hence it is zero. ◻

**Theorem.** If $F$ is a field, every ideal of $F[x]$ is principal.

*Proof.* For a nonzero ideal $I$, choose a monic $d\in I$ of least degree. Division writes every $f\in I$ as $f=qd+r$ with $\deg r<\deg d$; since $r\in I$, minimality gives $r=0$. Thus $I=(d)$. The zero ideal is $(0)$. ◻

## Quotient rings

**Theorem.** **Quotient ring construction.** If $I\trianglelefteq R$, then $R/I$ is a ring under

$$
(a+I)+(b+I)=a+b+I,
 \qquad (a+I)(b+I)=ab+I.
$$

The natural map $\pi:R\to R/I$, $a\mapsto a+I$, is surjective and has kernel $I$.

**Theorem.** **First isomorphism theorem for rings.** For a ring homomorphism $\phi:R\to S$,

$$
R/\ker\phi\cong\operatorname{im}\phi.
$$

*Proof.* The map $r+\ker\phi\mapsto\phi\(r\)$ is well-defined, injective, and surjective onto $\operatorname{im}\phi$, and it respects both ring operations and the identity. ◻

**Theorem.** **Correspondence theorem for ideals.** If $\phi:R\to S$ is surjective, $I\mapsto\phi(I)$ is an inclusion-preserving bijection between ideals $I\trianglelefteq R$ containing $\ker\phi$ and ideals of $S$. Its inverse is $J\mapsto\phi^{-1}(J)$.

*Proof.* The mutually inverse correspondences are

$$
I\longmapsto\phi(I),\qquad J\longmapsto\phi^{-1}(J),
$$

with

$$
\phi^{-1}(\phi(I))=I,\qquad \phi(\phi^{-1}(J))=J
$$

on the stated domains. ◻

## Adjoining elements

**Definition.** If $S$ is a ring containing $R$ and $a\in S$, then $R[a]$ is the smallest subring of $S$ containing $R$ and $a$. When $R$ and $S$ are commutative,

$$
R[a]=\\\{f(a):f\in R[x]\\\}.
$$

**Example.** **The coefficient ring is preserved.** In

$$
A=\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2-2),
$$

the coefficient map $\mathbb Z\to A$ is injective because $x^2-2$ is monic. Thus $A$ contains an isomorphic copy of $\mathbb Z$ (and a root of $x^2-2$).

**Example.** **The coefficient ring is destroyed.** Let $R=\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ and $A=R[x]/(2x-1)$. In $A$ one has $2x=1$; multiplication by $2$ gives $2=0$, and then $2x=1$ gives $1=0$. Hence $A$ is the zero ring and the coefficient map $R\to A$ is not injective.

The evaluation map $R[x]\to S$, $f\mapsto f(a)$, has image $R[a]$, hence $R[a]\cong R[x]/\ker(\operatorname{ev}_a)$. In an abstract construction $R[x]/(f)$, the coefficient map $R\to R[x]/(f)$ is injective exactly when $(f)\cap R=0$; a monic nonconstant $f$ is a useful sufficient condition.

## Product rings and idempotents

The Cartesian product $R\times S$ is a ring under componentwise operations.

**Definition.** An element $e\in R$ is *idempotent* if $e^2=e$. It is *central* if $er=re$ for every $r\in R$.

**Theorem.** **Decomposition by an idempotent.** If $e$ is a central idempotent of $R$, then $eR$ and $(1-e)R$ are ideals and rings with identities $e$ and $1-e$, and

$$
R\cong eR\times(1-e)R,
 \qquad r\longmapsto(er,(1-e)r).
$$

For a commutative ring every idempotent is central.

*Proof.* Centrality makes both summands two-sided ideals. The displayed map is a ring homomorphism. Its inverse is $(x,y)\mapsto x+y$: indeed $e(1-e)=0$ and every $r$ decomposes as $er+(1-e)r$. ◻

**Theorem.** **Chinese remainder theorem.** If $I,J\trianglelefteq R$ and $I+J=R$, then

$$
R\longrightarrow R/I\times R/J,
 \qquad r\longmapsto(r+I,r+J)
$$

is surjective with kernel $I\cap J$. Hence $R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J$.

*Proof.* Suppose $I+J=R$ and choose $i\in I$, $j\in J$ with $i+j=1$. For every $(a+I,b+J)\in R/I\times R/J$,

$$
aj+bi\longmapsto(a+I,b+J),
$$

because $j\equiv1\pmod I$ and $i\equiv1\pmod J$. Thus the map is surjective. ◻

## Fields of fractions

Let $R$ be an integral domain. On pairs $(a,b)$ with $b\ne0$, put $(a,b)\sim(c,d)$ when $ad=bc$. The class of $(a,b)$ is written $a/b$.

**Theorem.** **Field of fractions.** These classes form a field $\operatorname{Frac}\(R\)$ containing an isomorphic copy of $R$ via $a\mapsto a/1$.

*Proof.* Define $a/b+c/d=(ad+bc)/bd$ and $(a/b)(c/d)=ac/bd$. Every nonzero $a/b$ has inverse $b/a$, and $a\mapsto a/1$ is injective. ◻

**Theorem.** **Universal property.** If $\phi:R\to L$ is an injective homomorphism to a field, there is a unique homomorphism $\Phi:\operatorname{Frac}\(R\)\to L$ extending $\phi$, given by

$$
\Phi(a/b)=\phi(a)\phi(b)^{-1}.
$$

Thus every field containing $R$ contains a unique $R$-isomorphic copy of $\operatorname{Frac}\(R\)$.

*Proof.* Any extension must send $a/b=(a/1)(b/1)^{-1}$ to the displayed value, proving uniqueness. Cross multiplication and injectivity of $\phi$ prove the formula is well-defined; direct calculation proves it is a homomorphism. ◻

## Maximal and prime ideals

In this subsection $R$ is commutative.

**Definition.** A proper ideal $M$ is *maximal* if no proper ideal lies strictly between $M$ and $R$. A proper ideal $P$ is *prime* if $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$.

**Theorem.** An ideal $M$ is maximal if and only if $R/M$ is a field.

*Proof.* By the correspondence theorem, ideals of $R/M$ correspond to ideals of $R$ containing $M$. Thus $M$ is maximal exactly when the quotient has only $0$ and itself as ideals, which for a nonzero commutative ring is equivalent to every nonzero element being a unit. ◻

**Theorem.** An ideal $P$ is prime if and only if $R/P$ is an integral domain.

*Proof.* In $R/P$, $(a+P)(b+P)=0$ exactly when $ab\in P$. The absence of nonzero zero divisors is therefore exactly the defining prime-ideal implication. ◻
