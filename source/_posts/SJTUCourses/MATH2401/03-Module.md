Replacing a field by a ring removes division by nonzero scalars. Modules need not have bases, and submodules of free modules need not be direct summands. Throughout this article $R$ is a nonzero ring with identity.

## Module basics

**Definition.** A *left $R$-module* is an abelian group $(M,+)$ with an action $R\times M\to M$ satisfying

$$
\begin{aligned}
r(m+n)&=rm+rn, & (r+s)m&=rm+sm,\\\\
(rs)m&=r(sm), & 1m&=m.
\end{aligned}
$$

A *submodule* $N\le M$ is an additive subgroup closed under the action of $R$. The submodules of the left regular module ${}_RR$ are exactly the left ideals of $R$.

**Definition.** An $R$-linear map $f:M\to N$ preserves addition and satisfies $f(rm)=rf(m)$. Its kernel and image are submodules. Its cokernel is $\operatorname{coker} f=N/\operatorname{im} f$.

In particular, every $R$-linear map is a group homomorphism between the underlying additive groups of its modules.

**Definition.** For a submodule $N\le M$, the quotient $M/N$ has

$$
(m+N)+(m'+N)=m+m'+N,
\qquad r(m+N)=rm+N.
$$

Products and direct sums of modules use componentwise operations.

**Theorem.** **First isomorphism theorem for modules.** For an $R$-linear map $f:M\to N$,

$$
M/\ker f\cong\operatorname{im} f.
$$

## Linear operators as modules

Let $F$ be a field. If $V$ is an $F$-vector space and $T\in\operatorname{End}_F(V)$, define

$$
f(x)\cdot v=f(T)v\qquad(f\in F[x],\ v\in V).
$$

Conversely, restricting an $F[x]$-module to the constant polynomials gives an $F$-vector space and multiplication by $x$ gives an $F$-linear operator.

**Proposition.** The constructions above give an equivalence between $F[x]$-modules and pairs $(V,T)$ consisting of an $F$-vector space and an $F$-linear endomorphism. Moreover, $F[x]$-linear maps are exactly the $F$-linear maps commuting with the operators.

## Free modules and presentations

**Definition.** A subset $B\subseteq M$ is a *basis* if every $m\in M$ has a unique finite expression $m=\sum_{b\in B}r_bb$. A module with a basis is *free*. If $B=\\\{b_1,\dots,b_n\\\}$, then $M\cong R^n$.

For free modules with chosen finite bases, homomorphisms $R^n\to R^m$ are represented by $m\times n$ matrices (acting on column vectors). Over a noncommutative ring this convention is for left modules and coefficients must be placed consistently.

**Proposition.** Every module generated by $m_1,\dots,m_n$ is a quotient of $R^n$. More precisely, the map $(r_1,\dots,r_n)\mapsto\sum r_im_i$ is surjective, and its kernel is the module of relations among the generators.

Finite generation does not imply freeness: $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ is generated by one element as a $\mathbb Z$-module but is not free. A nonzero relation in one chosen presentation does not itself prove nonfreeness. For example,

$$
R^n/R(1,\dots,1)\cong R^{n-1};
$$

this presentation has a nonzero relation module although its quotient is free.

For nonzero commutative rings, any two bases of a finite free module have the same cardinality; this number is its *rank*. (Arbitrary noncommutative rings need not have this invariant-basis-number property.)

## Smith normal form

**Theorem.** **Smith normal form.** Let $R$ be a PID and $A\in M_{m\times n}\(R\)$. There are invertible matrices $P\in GL_m\(R\)$ and $Q\in GL_n\(R\)$ such that

$$
PAQ=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_r,0,\dots,0),
 \qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
$$

where every $d_i\ne0$. The nonzero $d_i$, normalized up to units, are uniquely determined and are called the invariant factors.

*Allowed reductions.* Smith reduction uses the following invertible operations, applied to either rows or columns:

1.  interchange two rows (or two columns);

2.  multiply a row (or column) by a unit of $R$;

3.  add a multiple of one row (or column) to another.

Equivalently, these operations replace $A$ by $PAQ$ with $P\in GL_m\(R\)$ and $Q\in GL_n\(R\)$. ◻

**Corollary.** Every subgroup of a free abelian group $\mathbb Z^m$ is free of rank at most $m$.

*Proof.* Induct on $m$, with $m=0$ immediate. Choose a coordinate projection $\pi:\mathbb Z^m\to\mathbb Z$. If $\pi(H)=0$, apply induction in $\ker\pi\cong\mathbb Z^{m-1}$. Otherwise $\pi(H)=d\mathbb Z$ for some $d>0$. Put $K=H\cap\ker\pi$ and choose $h_0\in H$ with $\pi(h_0)=d$. For $h\in H$, write $\pi(h)=qd$; then $h-qh_0\in K$, uniquely. Hence

$$
H=K\oplus\langle h_0\rangle\cong K\oplus\mathbb Z.
$$

Induction makes $K$ free of rank at most $m-1$, completing the proof. ◻

**Proposition.** Every finitely generated module $M$ over a PID has a finite presentation

$$
R^n\hookrightarrow R^m\twoheadrightarrow M\to0;
$$

equivalently, $M$ is the cokernel of an injective map between finite free modules.

*Proof.* For a surjection $\pi:R^m\twoheadrightarrow M$ and an isomorphism $\alpha:R^n\xrightarrow{\sim}\ker\pi$, the presentation is

$$
R^n\xrightarrow{\ \iota\circ\alpha\ }R^m
 \xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }M\longrightarrow0,
 \qquad \iota:\ker\pi\hookrightarrow R^m.
$$

 ◻

## Finitely generated abelian groups

**Theorem.** **Structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups.** Every finitely generated abelian group $A$ has a decomposition

$$
A\cong\mathbb Z^r\oplus\mathbb Z/n_1\mathbb Z\oplus\cdots
 \oplus\mathbb Z/n_k\mathbb Z,
 \qquad 1<n_1\mid n_2\mid\cdots\mid n_k.
$$

The rank $r$ and invariant factors $n_i$ are unique.

*Uniqueness.* The free rank is intrinsic:

$$
r=\dim_{\mathbb Q}(A\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Q).
$$

The torsion subgroup $T\le A$ and its $p$-primary subgroup $T_p$ are also intrinsic. If $e_{p,i}=v_p(n_i)$, then for every $j\ge1$,

$$
\dim_{\mathbb F_p}\bigl(p^{j-1}T_p/p^jT_p\bigr) =\\\#\\\{i:e_{p,i}\ge j\\\}.
$$

These dimensions recover the multiset of exponents $e_{p,i}$ for every prime $p$. Moreover, $k=\max_p\dim_{\mathbb F_p}(T_p/pT_p)$. Padding the exponent lists on the left with zeros to length $k$ and using $n_1\mid\cdots\mid n_k$ uniquely recovers each $n_i=\prod_p p^{e_{p,i}}$. ◻

The first summand is the free part and the finite direct sum is the torsion part. The torsion part can alternatively be decomposed into cyclic groups of prime-power order (the elementary-divisor form). Its exponent is $n_k$ when $k>0$, i.e. the least positive integer annihilating every torsion element.

## Application to linear operators

Since $F[x]$ is a PID, the structure theorem applies to the $F[x]$-module associated with a linear operator $T$.

**Theorem.** **Invariant-factor decomposition.** Let $V$ be finite-dimensional over $F$ and let $T\in\operatorname{End}_F(V)$. There are unique monic nonconstant polynomials $f_1\mid f_2\mid\cdots\mid f_k$ such that, as $F[x]$-modules,

$$
V\cong F[x]/(f_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus F[x]/(f_k).
$$

There is no free $F[x]$ summand, because $F[x]$ is infinite-dimensional over $F$. The companion matrices of the $f_i$ give the rational canonical form of $T$.
